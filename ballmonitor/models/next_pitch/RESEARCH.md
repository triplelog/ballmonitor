## Summary

- **Most accurate model: Average of GBM and transformer.** On the 2025–2026 test seasons (pitchers with ≥2,000 pitches in the season) it picked the right pitch type **44.9%** of the time vs **37.3%** for the control (the pitcher's historical mix in that count), and cut log loss by 0.385 bits per pitch (95% CI 0.379 to 0.391).
- **Shipped default: gradient boosting** (44.4%, 1.859 bits). It is within 0.012 bits of the best model and needs no neural network at run time.
- **The simple context tree** (`method = "tree"`, chain `batter side first, then count, previous pitch`) gets 40.8% accuracy, 0.257 bits per pitch better than the control. The learned model adds 0.129 bits and 4.0 accuracy points on top.
- **Pitch types remain mostly unpredictable.** The pitchers' own season mixes average 2.15 bits of uncertainty per pitch; the best model removes 0.30 bits of that (14%).
- **Hardest to predict:** Seth Lugo (2026, 2.62 bits, 26% accuracy); Seth Lugo (2025, 2.59 bits, 32% accuracy); Tomoyuki Sugano (2026, 2.50 bits, 32% accuracy); Sandy Alcantara (2026, 2.48 bits, 29% accuracy); Michael Lorenzen (2026, 2.37 bits, 35% accuracy).
- **Do surprising pitches work better?** Holding pitcher, season, pitch type and count fixed, each extra bit of surprise changes runs saved by +0.118 per 100 pitches (95% CI +0.039 to +0.188); called+swinging strike rate by +0.53 points (+0.37 to +0.71); whiffs per swing by +0.52 points (+0.30 to +0.76). The gain is concentrated in the most surprising fifth of pitches. See §6 for what this does and does not show.
- **Unpredictable pitchers are not better pitchers.** Across pitcher-seasons, wider mixes go with *fewer* whiffs per swing (Spearman -0.34) and no clear difference in runs saved (-0.12). Dominant two-pitch pitchers are the easiest to predict and among the best; being unpredictable helps a given pitch, not a pitcher without the stuff.

## How models were compared

- **Walk-forward.** Each pitch is predicted from pitches thrown before it; history tables update after every pitch (including earlier pitches in the same game). Learned models are trained only on seasons before the test season; the last 20% of the previous season's days are held out for early stopping.
- **Tuning** (context-tree half-life and smoothing strengths) used only 2024.
- **Scores:** accuracy (top pick right), log loss in bits per pitch (lower is better; 0 = certain and right), Brier score. Log loss is the main score because it rewards honest probabilities, not just the top pick.
- **Intervals:** 95% bootstrap over pitcher-games. Only two independent test seasons exist.

## 1b. Does each pitcher need their own learned model?

Every model here is per pitcher in the sense that it reads that pitcher's own history. The question is whether the *learned* part should also be fitted separately for each pitcher (a small boosted model per pitcher, trained on their pitches only) or shared across all pitchers. Compared on the 384,311 test pitches of 108 focus pitchers with enough history for their own model:

Own minus shared: +0.088 [+0.085, +0.091] bits per pitch (negative = own models better).

## 7. Caveats

- Statcast pitch labels change over time (e.g. sweepers, slurves) and are algorithmic; a pitch that is relabelled looks 'new' until history builds up again (recency weighting helps).
- Focus pitchers are mostly starters; relievers with small samples are less predictable from history.
- Within-game history is used as it happens, which assumes the pitch type of each pitch is known immediately (true for a live feed).
- The neural and boosted models were given a single set of hyper-parameters, not a search; they might improve with tuning (the context tree was tuned on 2024).
